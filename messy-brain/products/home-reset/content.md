# Hi, Messy Brain. Welcome in.
<!--meta
id: welcome
kind: plain
color: lilac
kicker: A quick hello (2-minute read)
-->

If your home looks like a tornado that got bored halfway through, and you've spent more hours *feeling bad* about the mess than cleaning it, you're in the right place.

This book is not going to tell you to wake up at 5 a.m., color-code your spices, or "clean as you go." (If "clean as you go" worked for us, we'd already be doing it.)

Instead, you'll get **a small, forgiving system built for how our brains actually work:** we lose track of what we can't see, we struggle to start, we get tired from tiny decisions, we have no idea how long anything takes, and we tend to do things all the way or not at all.

That's not a flaw you need to fix before you can have a nice home. **It's the design brief.**

## What this book promises (and what it doesn't)

:::win What you'll get
- A home that's **functional most days**, not spotless some days
- A plan for **the next 7 days**, with a tiny version for rough days
- Systems that **catch the mess before it becomes a disaster**
- A way back in, every single time you fall off
:::

What you won't get: a perfect home. Perfect homes are for magazines and people with staff. We're going for **"I could have a friend over with 15 minutes' notice and not want to move to another country."**

# Why Normal Cleaning Advice Fails Our Brains
<!--meta
id: why
color: lilac
tagline: It was never a character flaw. It was a design flaw.
one: Most cleaning advice assumes you can **see** mess, **start** easily, **decide** fast, **feel** time passing, and happily do things **halfway.** If those are hard for you, the advice was built for someone else. This book builds around those five things instead of fighting them.
-->

Here's a secret the tidy people never told us: most cleaning advice is written by people whose brains quietly do half the work for them. Their brains *notice* the mail pile. Their brains *nudge* them to start. Their brains *decide* where the scissors go without a committee meeting.

Many of us with ADHD don't get those free nudges. So we read the same advice, try it, crash, and decide the problem is us.

**It isn't.** Let's translate.

Now let's meet the five brain things that make housework feel harder for us. Knowing them is half the fix, because once you know *why* the mail piles up, you can stop taking it personally and start designing around it.

## 1. Out of sight, out of mind

Lots of ADHD folks describe it the same way: **if I can't see it, it stops existing.** The clean towels in the closet? Gone. The bill in the drawer? Never heard of it. The produce in the crisper drawer? Now a science project.

And the flip side: the stuff we *can* see, we stop seeing after a few days. The mail pile blends into the wallpaper. That's why "put things away" advice backfires. Put away often means *disappear*.

:::brain Brain note
This is why people with messy-brain homes often have **piles instead of drawers.** Piles are a clever workaround: they keep things visible. The problem isn't the pile instinct. It's that piles have no system. We'll give them one.
:::

**The fix:** visible storage (clear bins, hooks, open baskets, labels), and **reset points** that make you look at your space on purpose.

## 2. The starting problem

You know the feeling. You *want* to do the dishes. You're standing right there. And somehow your body won't start. It's like there's an invisible wall of awful between you and the task.

That wall is real, and it's not about willpower. For a lot of us, **starting is the hardest part of any task**, far harder than the task itself. Once we're moving, we're often fine. Sometimes we're even great.

**The fix:** make the start ridiculously small and oddly specific. Not "clean the kitchen." Instead: *"put five forks in the dishwasher."* Plus a timer, so the brain knows there's an end. Plus company (a body double), so it's easier to begin.

:::try Try this now
Say out loud, the smallest possible start of a task you're avoiding: *"I'm going to carry one mug to the kitchen."* Then do only that. Notice what happens next. (Often: a second mug.)
:::

## 3. Decision fatigue

Every object on a messy counter is a tiny question. *Keep it? Where does it go? Is that where it goes? Should I have a place for this? What even is this?*

Fifty objects is fifty questions. By question twelve, the brain taps out and suddenly you're on your phone, or reorganizing a drawer that wasn't even the problem.

**The fix:** make decisions *once*, in advance, so tidying becomes putting things where they already go. The 5-bin method (Chapter 7) and the decision scripts in this book exist so you never have to argue with a sock again.

## 4. Time blindness

Our internal clocks are... creative. A task we hate feels like it will take three hours. A task we love eats three hours and feels like ten minutes.

So "unload the dishwasher" gets filed as a Big Awful Job, when for many of us it takes around five minutes. And "I'll quickly tidy before I leave" becomes being late. Again.

**The fix:** timers everywhere, and a fun little experiment called **"Time it once."** Time the tasks you dread. Write the real number down. The dread usually shrinks when you see it.

:::try Try this now
Next time you unload the dishwasher or fold a load of laundry, start a stopwatch. Write the time on a sticky note and put it where you do the task. It becomes a tiny argument-winner for future you: *"It's only 6 minutes."*
:::

## 5. All-or-nothing

Our brains love extremes. Either the whole house gets cleaned top to bottom in a heroic, snack-fueled 9-hour sprint... or nothing happens for three weeks.

The problem is that the heroic sprint is so exhausting that the brain files cleaning under *"never again."* And "a little bit" feels pointless, so we skip it.

**The fix:** every task in this book has a **minimum version** that still counts. Doing 10% is not failing. **Doing 10% is the system working.**

## Bonus: the boredom wall

Housework is repetitive, and many of our brains run on interest, novelty, urgency, or challenge more than on importance. That's why you can clean the whole kitchen when a friend is 20 minutes away (urgency!) but not on a quiet Tuesday.

**The fix:** borrow the good stuff on purpose. Race a song. Set a "beat the timer" challenge. Put on a podcast you only allow yourself during cleaning. Clean while on the phone with a friend. We're not above bribing ourselves. We're smart enough to.

## The shame loop (and where we break it)

<div class="loop-step">The mess grows</div>
<div class="loop-arrow">&rarr;</div>
<div class="loop-step">We feel ashamed</div>
<div class="loop-arrow">&rarr;</div>
<div class="loop-step">Shame makes starting even harder</div>
<div class="loop-arrow">&rarr;</div>
<div class="loop-step">We avoid it</div>
<div class="loop-arrow">&#8635;</div>

Shame feels like motivation, but it works like a brake. The more awful you feel about the kitchen, the harder it is to walk into the kitchen.

So we break the loop at the shame part. Not by pretending the mess isn't there, but by treating it like what it is: **information.** A pile by the door means "stuff needs a home by the door." A clothes chair means "worn-once clothes need a home." Mess is your house telling you where the systems are missing.

:::script Say it like this (to yourself)
*"This isn't a character problem. It's a systems problem. And systems can be fixed in 15 minutes at a time."*
:::

# The 6 House Rules
<!--meta
id: rules
color: butter
tagline: Six little rules that quietly do the heavy lifting.
one: **Reset, don't deep clean. Visible beats hidden. Every thing gets a home, every home gets a label. Done beats perfect. Touch it once. Never clean alone without a timer.** If you remember nothing else, remember these six.
-->

These rules are the backbone of everything else in this book. You don't need to memorize them. They'll keep showing up until they feel obvious. There's also a copy on the printable Quick Start card at the back.

## Rule 1: Reset, don't deep clean

A **reset** means putting a space back to *usable*: counters clear enough to cook, floor clear enough to walk, bed clear enough to sleep. That's the goal. Not sparkling. Usable.

Deep cleaning (scrubbing grout, wiping baseboards, organizing the linen closet) is nice. It's also a trap for our all-or-nothing brains, because it's huge, and it makes the everyday stuff feel pointless by comparison.

**What it looks like:** you walk into the kitchen at 9 p.m. and instead of thinking "this whole kitchen is disgusting," you think "reset: dishes in, counters clear, wipe, done."

:::brain Brain note
A clean-enough home every day beats a perfect home once a month. Deep cleaning can happen later, a little at a time, once the resets are easy. Most weeks, the resets *are* the cleaning.
:::

## Rule 2: Visible beats hidden

If out of sight is out of mind, then **in sight is in mind.** So we design for seeing.

- **Open baskets beat lidded bins.** A lid is one more step, and one more wall to hide behind.
- **Hooks beat hangers.** Hanging a coat on a hook is one move. A hanger is four.
- **Clear containers beat opaque ones**, especially in the fridge and pantry.
- **Open shelves beat deep drawers** for things you use often.
- **Keep one surface empty on purpose** so you notice when it fills up.

:::try Try this now
Find one closed thing that keeps getting ignored: a drawer, a lidded bin, a cabinet. Take the lid off, or move its contents to an open basket. Done. That's a whole system upgrade.
:::

## Rule 3: Every thing gets a home, every home gets a label

Mess is mostly **things without homes.** Nobody leaves their toothbrush on the living room floor, because the toothbrush has a very clear home. The chaos lives in the in-between stuff: chargers, hair ties, tote bags, the weird cable.

Two tricks make homes work for us:

1. **Put the home where the thing already lands.** If your keys always end up on the kitchen counter, the key home goes *on the kitchen counter.* Don't fight the landing spot. Upgrade it.
2. **Label the home, not the thing.** A label on a basket that says "CHARGERS" does your remembering for you. Labels also help everyone else in your house put stuff back (more on that in Chapter 11).

:::brain Brain note
If a home is hard to reach (behind a door, under another thing, requires a step stool), the floor will become the home. **Easy beats pretty.** A basket on the floor labeled "SHOES" works better than a gorgeous shoe cabinet you never open.
:::

## Rule 4: Done beats perfect

Perfectionism and ADHD are old friends. It sounds like: *"If I can't do the whole thing properly, why bother?"* And then nothing gets done.

Around here we aim for **the B-minus clean.** Dishes in the dishwasher, even if it's not loaded like a Tetris champion. Laundry in a basket, even if it's not folded. A bed with the duvet pulled up, even if the pillows are a mess.

**B-minus, every day, beats A-plus, never.**

:::script Say it like this
*"Good enough is the goal. It's not settling. It's the strategy."*
:::

## Rule 5: Touch it once

When you pick something up, **take it to its home, or to a basket that's headed there.** Try not to put it down "for now." *For now* is how piles are born.

We're realistic about this, though. Sometimes the thing's home is upstairs and you're in the middle of something. So we use a **"Going That Way" basket**: a basket that collects stuff for another room. When you're headed that way, the basket goes with you.

- Upstairs? A basket at the bottom of the stairs.
- Car? A tote by the door.
- Other rooms? One basket in the main room.

**Rule of the basket:** it gets emptied once a day, usually during your Daily 3 (page {{p:daily3}}).

## Rule 6: Body double + timer

Two tools that turn "I can't start" into "oh, I'm doing it":

**A timer** tells your brain the suffering has an end. "Clean the kitchen" is endless. "Clean for 12 minutes" is survivable. A visual timer (one where you can see the time shrinking) works especially well for a lot of us.

**A body double** is another person nearby while you do the task. They don't need to help. They can be:

- A friend on a video call, doing their own chores
- A partner reading on the couch in the same room
- A "clean with me" video playing on your phone
- A virtual coworking room
- Honestly, sometimes the dog counts

:::brain Brain note
Nobody fully agrees on why body doubling works so well for so many of us, but plenty of ADHD folks swear by it. Maybe it's gentle accountability. Maybe it's that another person makes the room feel "on." Either way: it's free, and it's worth a try.
:::

## The rule under all the rules

**Be kind to the person doing the cleaning.** That's you. You'll get more done as your own friend than as your own drill sergeant. Every time.

# The 15-Minute Emergency Reset
<!--meta
id: emergency
color: coral
tagline: Someone's coming over. Don't panic. Grab a basket.
one: **Only clean what they'll see.** Walk the "guest path" (door, sitting spot, bathroom). Trash in a bag, everything else in one big basket, basket out of sight. Dishes in the sink, bathroom wiped, lamps on. Fifteen minutes. Then empty the basket within 48 hours so it doesn't become a new doom pile.
-->

Your phone buzzes. *"Hey! I'm in the area, can I pop by in 20?"* Your heart drops into your socks. You look around. Every flat surface is wearing a hat of stuff.

This is the plan for that moment. It's also a great plan for any day you want fast, visible progress, because it's built on the one thing our brains do brilliantly: **urgency.**

## What you need (grab these first, 30 seconds)

- **One trash bag**
- **One big "chaos basket"** (a laundry basket, a big tote, or a large bag)
- **Disinfecting wipes** or a damp cloth with a bit of soap
- **Your phone** with a 15-minute timer and a fast song

## The plan, minute by minute

<table class="timer">
<thead><tr><th>Time</th><th>What you do</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>0:00&ndash;1:00</td><td><strong>Walk the guest path.</strong> Stand at the front door and look in, like a visitor. Where will they walk? Where will they sit? Will they use the bathroom? That path is the <em>only</em> area you're cleaning.</td></tr>
<tr><td>1:00&ndash;4:00</td><td><strong>Trash sweep.</strong> Trash bag in hand, walk the guest path and grab obvious trash only. Cups, wrappers, boxes, mail envelopes.</td></tr>
<tr><td>4:00&ndash;8:00</td><td><strong>Chaos basket sweep.</strong> Everything that isn't trash and isn't where it lives goes in the basket. Floor first, then surfaces. Don't sort. Don't deliver. Basket goes in a bedroom, closet, or car trunk.</td></tr>
<tr><td>8:00&ndash;10:00</td><td><strong>Dishes.</strong> Everything into the sink or dishwasher. Sink overflowing? Fill it with hot soapy water. A soaking sink looks <em>intentional.</em></td></tr>
<tr><td>10:00&ndash;12:00</td><td><strong>Bathroom blitz.</strong> Swish the toilet, wipe the seat and rim, wipe the sink. Fresh hand towel. Toilet paper within reach. Close the shower curtain. Done.</td></tr>
<tr><td>12:00&ndash;14:00</td><td><strong>Wipe the sitting spot.</strong> Wipe the table they'll sit at. Fluff the couch cushions. Fold one blanket over the arm of the couch (instant "cozy on purpose").</td></tr>
<tr><td>14:00&ndash;15:00</td><td><strong>Set the mood.</strong> Turn on lamps (warm light is very forgiving). Turn off harsh overhead lights. Open a window for a minute. Put on music. Breathe.</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

:::brain Why this works
Guests don't see your home the way you do. You see every pile and remember every unfinished project. They see a lamp, a couch, and a friend. **The guest path is maybe a fifth of your home**, and that's all they'll notice.
:::

## The 5-minute version (they're already parking)

1. **Chaos basket** on the floor and the coffee table. Basket into a bedroom. Close the door.
2. **Dishes** into the sink, hot soapy water on top.
3. **Toilet** swish, seat wipe, fresh hand towel.
4. **Lamps on.**

That's it. You're a host now.

## What NOT to do in an emergency

- **Don't start anything you might get stuck in.** No organizing drawers, no sorting mail, no "oh, I'll quickly fix this shelf." Emergencies are for hiding, not organizing.
- **Don't vacuum unless the floor is visibly crunchy.** It's loud, slow, and guests rarely notice floors.
- **Don't clean rooms they won't enter.** Close those doors with pride.
- **Don't apologize for your home when they walk in.** It makes guests feel like they need to reassure you, and it makes you feel worse.

:::script Say it like this (instead of apologizing)
*"Come in! Want tea?"*

That's the whole script. If you *really* need to say something: *"Welcome to our lived-in home!"* Cheerful, done, moving on.
:::

## Aftercare: the 48-hour basket rule

Here's the catch: the chaos basket is **not a home.** If it sits in the bedroom for a month, it becomes a doom pile with handles.

So within 48 hours:

- [ ] Set a 10-minute timer.
- [ ] Tip the basket out onto a bed or clear floor.
- [ ] Use the 5-bin method (page {{p:declutter}}) or walk each thing to its home.
- [ ] Whatever's left when the timer rings goes back in the basket for one more round tomorrow.
- [ ] Basket back in its spot, empty and ready for next time.

:::try Try this now
Choose your emergency chaos basket today and give it a permanent spot. Put a sticky note inside: *"Empty me within 48 hours."* Future you, with a guest arriving in 15 minutes, will be very grateful.
:::

# The 7-Day Reset
<!--meta

tagline: One zone a day. One week. A home that works again.

Welcome to the main event. Over the next seven days, we'll reset the parts of your home that cause the most daily chaos, and put tiny systems in place so they stay reset.

## How the 7-Day Reset works

**One zone a day.** Not the whole house. Your brain gets one job each day, and a finish line.

**Timed rounds.** Each day is split into rounds of 10 to 15 minutes with short breaks. You can do all the rounds at once, spread them through the day, or stop after one.

**Three versions of every day:**

<div class="versions">
<div class="v v-min"><b>Minimum</b><span>10 minutes or less. For low-energy days. Fully counts.</span></div>
<div class="v v-std"><b>Standard</b><span>About 45 to 60 minutes in rounds. The full reset.</span></div>
<div class="v v-bonus"><b>Bonus</b><span>Only if you're on a roll. Totally optional.</span></div>
</div>

**The order matters (a bit).** We start with the kitchen because it changes the mood of the whole house fastest. Then we move through the spots where daily life gets stuck. But if one zone is on fire, it's okay to swap days around.

## The rules for this week

- **Skipping is allowed.** Missing a day is not failing the week. It's a normal week for a human.
- **Never double up.** Missed Day 3? Tomorrow is Day 4's minimum version, not a Day 3 + Day 4 marathon. (You can come back to Day 3 later, or never.)
- **Take "before" photos.** Our brains forget progress fast. Photos are proof.
- **Stop when the timer rings.** Even if you're on a roll. Especially if you're on a roll. Ending while it still feels okay makes tomorrow easier to start.
- **Celebrate every day.** Tick the box, text a friend, eat the good snack. Your brain learns from rewards, not lectures.

## Day 0: Gather your kit {.day}

<p class="day-meta"><span>15 minutes</span><span>Do this the day before Day 1</span></p>

Before we start, gather a small "reset kit." This is the difference between *"I'll start... wait, where are the trash bags?"* (and suddenly it's 45 minutes later and you're reorganizing the junk drawer) and actually starting.

**Your reset kit:**

- [ ] **A roll of trash bags**
- [ ] **One laundry basket** (your chaos basket / "Going That Way" basket)
- [ ] **Five boxes, bags, or bins** for decluttering, labeled: TRASH, DONATE, ELSEWHERE, KEEP, MAYBE (more on these on page {{p:declutter}})
- [ ] **A marker and some sticky notes or masking tape** for labels
- [ ] **Wipes or a spray bottle and cloth**
- [ ] **A timer** (your phone is fine; a visual timer is great)
- [ ] **A playlist** of fast, happy songs, or a podcast you like
- [ ] **This book**, and a pen

Put everything in one basket or tote, somewhere you'll see it tomorrow. That's your **reset caddy** for the week.

:::min Minimum version
Find **one basket** and **one trash bag**. Put them by the kitchen. You're ready.
:::

## Day 2: The Living Room (floor & couch zone) {.day}

<p class="day-meta"><span>Standard: 3 rounds of 12 min</span><span>Minimum: 10 min</span></p>

**Today's goal:** a clear floor and a clear coffee table.

# The Daily 3

The 7-Day Reset got your home back. The Daily 3 keeps it. It's the smallest amount of daily effort that stops the mess from snowballing.

No schedules. No 20-item chore charts. Three named resets, each 10 minutes or less.

## Meet your Daily 3

<div class="three">
<div class="three-card c1"><span class="n">1</span><b>The Kitchen Closing Shift</b><p>Dishes in, counters clear, sink empty. <em>About 10 min.</em></p></div>
<div class="three-card c2"><span class="n">2</span><b>The 10-Minute Tidy</b><p>Floor and surfaces of your main room. <em>10 min, timer on.</em></p></div>
<div class="three-card c3"><span class="n">3</span><b>Tomorrow-You Prep</b><p>Launch pad check and one thing set out. <em>2 min.</em></p></div>
</div>

## Reset 1: The Kitchen Closing Shift

Restaurants don't close by "cleaning as they go." They have a **closing shift**: a short, fixed list done every night. Your kitchen gets one too.

1. **Dishes in** the dishwasher or rack (or stacked by the sink, crusty ones soaking).
2. **Counters cleared** of anything that isn't a daily-use item.
3. **Wipe** the counters and stovetop.
4. **Sink empty.** Run the dishwasher if it's full.
5. **Close the kitchen.** Hang a fresh towel, turn off the light. Kitchen's closed.

The full system (with a troubleshooting section for tiny kitchens and big families) is on page {{p:systems}}.

## Reset 2: The 10-Minute Tidy

Pick the room you spend the most time in. Set a 10-minute timer. Then, in this order:

1. **Trash** out
2. **Dishes** to the kitchen
3. **Floor** clear (floor first, it has the biggest visual impact)
4. **Surfaces** clear-ish: stuff back to its home, or into the "Going That Way" basket
5. **Deliver** the basket, if there's time

When the timer rings: **stop.** Even mid-pile. The goal isn't a finished room. It's a room that never gets more than a day's worth of messy.

## Reset 3: Tomorrow-You Prep

Two minutes at the launch pad, ideally before bed or while the kettle boils:

- **Keys, wallet, phone charger** in their spots?
- **Bag** packed for tomorrow?
- **Anything that needs to leave the house** in the OUT basket?
- **One thing set out** for the morning (clothes, gym bag, lunch container)?

That's it. Morning you will feel like someone looked after them. Because someone did.

## Stack them onto things you already do

The Daily 3 works best when it's glued to a habit you already have, so you don't have to *remember* it. Some ideas:

<thead><tr><th>Reset</th><th>Glue it to...</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Kitchen Closing Shift</td><td>Right after dinner, before you sit down. Or while the kettle boils for your evening tea.</td></tr>
<tr><td>10-Minute Tidy</td><td>Before you turn on the TV. During the first ten minutes of a podcast. When the kids start bath time.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Tomorrow-You Prep</td><td>After brushing your teeth. When you plug in your phone at night.</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

:::try Try this now
Write your three resets on a sticky note, **each with its glue.** For example: *"After dinner: kitchen closing shift. Before TV: 10-min tidy. After teeth: launch pad."* Stick it on the fridge at eye level.
:::

## Make it your own

The default Daily 3 works for a lot of people, but your home might need a different three. Swap in whatever causes the most chaos in *your* house:

- **Laundry chaos?** Swap Reset 2 for *"one load, all the way"* (start it in the morning, put it away at night).
- **Kids' toys everywhere?** Make Reset 2 a *"toy rescue"*: everything back in bins before bath time, set to one song.
- **Pets?** Add a 2-minute floor sweep in the spot where the fur gathers.
- **Work from home?** Swap Reset 3 for *"close the office"*: clear the desk, shut the laptop, push in the chair.

## How we count (the streak that doesn't break)

Here's the scoring system:

- **3 out of 3:** a great day.
- **2 out of 3:** a great day.
- **1 out of 3:** still a great day.
- **0 out of 3:** a day off. Tomorrow is a new day. No catching up.

Track **days with at least one reset**, not perfect days. That's a streak that bends instead of breaks.

:::min The Daily 1 (for rough days)
On days when everything is hard, do only the **Kitchen Closing Shift's first step: dishes in the sink or dishwasher.** That's it. It keeps the most important room from sliding, and it keeps your streak alive. The Daily 1 fully counts.
:::

:::brain Brain note
Daily systems often get boring for our brains after a couple of weeks. That's not you failing. It's novelty wearing off. When it happens, change the *flavor*, not the system: new playlist, new timer, a different body double, a race against a song, a "one-podcast-episode" rule. Same three resets, fresh packaging.
:::

# Systems That Run Themselves
<!--meta

This chapter is a toolbox. You don't need all seven systems at once. **Pick the one that fixes your most annoying daily problem** and start there. Each one follows the same format:

- **The problem** (you'll recognize it)
- **The system** (how it works)
- **Set it up** (15 minutes or less)
- **Keep it going** (tiny daily habit)
- **When it breaks** (because it will, and that's fine)

## System 1: The Launch Pad {.sys}

**The problem:** You lose your keys, wallet, or phone at least twice a week. Stuff you bring home spreads through the house. The mornings are chaos.

**The system:** one spot, right where you walk in, where everything that goes in and out of the house lives. Your house's airport gate.

**Set it up (15 minutes):**

- [ ] **Find the landing spot.** Where does your stuff *already* land when you walk in? That's where the launch pad goes. Even if it's the kitchen counter.
- [ ] **A bowl or tray** for keys, wallet, sunglasses, earbuds.
- [ ] **A hook** (or two) for your bag and coat. Hooks, not hangers.
- [ ] **A charging spot** for your phone, if there's an outlet nearby.
- [ ] **An OUT basket** for anything that needs to leave the house: returns, borrowed stuff, library books, things to mail.
- [ ] **A shoe zone:** a tray, a mat, or a basket.
- [ ] **Labels on everything.** KEYS. OUT. SHOES.

**Keep it going:** every time you walk in, **everything in hand goes onto the pad before you do anything else.** Every night, the 2-minute Tomorrow-You Prep (page {{p:daily3}}).

**When it breaks:** if the pad keeps overflowing, it's too small or too far from the door. Move it closer. Add a second hook. And if stuff lands somewhere else consistently, *move the pad there.* Your habits are the boss.

:::brain Brain note
A launch pad works because it removes the "where does this go?" decision at the exact moment you're most distracted: walking in the door, arms full, already thinking about dinner.
:::

## System 2: The Laundry Loop {.sys}

**The problem:** Wet clothes forgotten in the washer (again). A clean-laundry mountain on the couch. Nothing to wear, but a lot of clothes.

**The system:** **one load, all the way, in one day,** with alarms doing the remembering for you.

**Set it up (15 minutes):**

- [ ] **A hamper where clothes come off.** Usually the bedroom and the bathroom. Not in a closet with a door.
- [ ] **Two hampers (or one divided one):** darks and lights. That's all the sorting you'll ever need to do.
- [ ] **No-fold bins or drawers** for underwear, socks, pajamas, gym clothes, kids' clothes, towels.
- [ ] **Hangers near the dryer or rack**, so shirts go straight onto hangers, skipping the folding step.
- [ ] **A "laundry day" alarm on your phone**, if you like fixed days.

**Keep it going, the loop in five beats:**

1. **Start** a load when a hamper is full (morning is great).
2. **Alarm** named "WET CLOTHES" the moment you press start.
3. **Switch** to the dryer or rack. New alarm: "DRY CLOTHES".
4. **Hang or toss** straight into no-fold bins. Fold only what really needs it.
5. **Put away.** The load isn't done until it's in the drawer.

**When it breaks:** clean-laundry mountain again? Don't fold it. Sort it into people or categories into baskets, and treat those baskets as "drawers" for the week. Then restart the loop with *one* load. Also: if the washer is far away (a shared basement, a laundromat), pick a fixed day and pair it with a podcast or a friend.

:::try Try this now
Open your phone's alarm app and create two alarms, turned off, named **WET CLOTHES** and **DRY CLOTHES**. Next time you start a load, turn one on. No remembering needed.
:::

## System 3: The Kitchen Closing Shift {.sys}

**The problem:** You wake up to last night's dishes. Cooking breakfast means cleaning first. The kitchen sets the mood for the whole day, and the mood is "ugh."

**The system:** a fixed 5-step closing shift, done at the same point every evening.

**Set it up (10 minutes):**

- [ ] **Clear the "dish drop" spot:** the counter next to the sink. That's where dirty dishes wait during the day.
- [ ] **Soap, a brush, and a cloth** within arm's reach of the sink.
- [ ] **An empty dishwasher** (or drying rack) to start. An empty dishwasher is a place for dirty dishes to go all day instead of the sink.
- [ ] **Tape the 5 steps** on the inside of a cabinet door.

**The closing shift:**

1. Dishes in
2. Counters cleared
3. Counters and stovetop wiped
4. Sink empty, dishwasher running if full
5. "Kitchen closed": fresh towel, light off

**Keep it going:** glue it to the end of dinner, and **unload the dishwasher in the morning** while the coffee brews. (Time it once. It's often under five minutes.)

**When it breaks:** skipped a few nights and now it's a disaster? Do the **Day 1 kitchen plan** (page {{p:seven}}) once, then go back to the closing shift. Tiny kitchen, no dishwasher? Wash in batches: all cups, then all plates, then all cutlery. Big family? Split the steps: someone clears, someone loads, someone wipes.

## System 4: Dismantling Doom Piles {.sys}

**The problem:** Piles. On the dining table, the stairs, the corner of the bedroom. They grow quietly. They're full of mystery items, a few important papers, and a lot of guilt.

**The system:** a fast, no-thinking method to dismantle one pile at a time, and a legal "doom basket" to stop new ones.

**Set it up:** grab your 5 bins (TRASH, DONATE, ELSEWHERE, KEEP, MAYBE) and a timer.

**The method: top-down, ten at a time.**

1. **Set a 10-minute timer.** One pile. Never "all the piles."
2. **Start from the top.** Pick up one thing.
3. **Decide in 10 seconds** (use the scripts on page {{p:declutter}}). Drop it in a bin.
4. **No side quests.** No reading letters, no testing pens, no trying things on. Anything that needs more than 10 seconds goes to MAYBE.
5. **Every 10 items, pause** and take the trash bag out if it's full. Movement helps.
6. **When the timer rings: stop.** Date the MAYBE box. Deliver ELSEWHERE.

:::brain The legal doom basket
Doom piles happen because our brains need somewhere to put things *right now* when there's no obvious home. So give yourself one **official doom basket** per floor. It's allowed to be messy. The only rule: it gets emptied once a week, during the Sunday Reset (page {{p:sunday}}). A contained doom basket beats five wild doom piles.
:::

**When it breaks:** if the same kind of item keeps showing up in your piles (chargers, receipts, kids' art), that item needs a home. Make one, label it, and that pile shrinks forever.

## System 5: Paper & Mail {.sys}

**The problem:** Mail piles up. Bills get lost. You've paid at least one late fee purely because an envelope blended into the counter.

**The system:** open mail standing over the recycling bin, and sort into **three visible folders.** That's all.

**Set it up (15 minutes):**

- [ ] **A standing file or wall pocket** near where mail lands (visible beats hidden, remember).
- [ ] **Three folders, bright colors, big labels:**
  **ACT** (forms, appointments, things to do), **PAY** (bills), **KEEP** (important: tax stuff, warranties, records).
- [ ] **A recycling bin right there**, and a bag for anything to shred.
- [ ] **A pen and sticky notes** clipped nearby.

**Keep it going:**

- **Daily:** open the mail over the recycling bin. Junk goes straight in. Everything else goes in a folder. Two minutes.
- **Weekly, during the Sunday Reset:** a 10-minute "paper date." Pay what's in PAY. Do or schedule what's in ACT. Move KEEP items into a real file box (or snap a photo if a digital copy is enough).
- **Switch to paperless** for any bill you can. One less envelope to lose.

**When it breaks:** if the folders overflow, you've skipped a few paper dates. No problem. Set a timer for 15 minutes and deal with PAY first. Bills with dates come before everything else.

:::try Try this now
Walk to your mail pile with a recycling bag. Set a timer for 5 minutes. Recycle every obvious piece of junk mail, unopened. Watch the pile shrink by half.
:::

## System 6: The Bathroom 2-Minute Wipe {.sys}

**The problem:** The bathroom goes from fine to "please don't come in here" surprisingly fast. Cleaning it feels like a Big Job, so it waits until it really is one.

**The system:** a tiny wipe every day, with supplies that live **in the bathroom**, in plain sight.

**Set it up (10 minutes):**

- [ ] **Wipes or a spray and cloth** under the sink or in a caddy on the counter.
- [ ] **A toilet brush with a holder** next to the toilet, plus a toilet cleaner that stays in the room.
- [ ] **A spare roll of toilet paper** where you can reach it from the toilet. (A basket works.)
- [ ] **A small trash can with a liner** and spare liners stored at the bottom of the can.
- [ ] **A hook for the towel,** not a rail. Hooks are easier.

**The 2-minute wipe (after evening teeth-brushing):**

1. Wipe the sink and faucet
2. Wipe the toilet seat and rim, then flush
3. Toothpaste, brush, and stuff back in their spots

**Keep it going:** glue it to brushing your teeth. Once a week, during the Sunday Reset, swap the towels and swish the toilet bowl.

**When it breaks:** skipped a week? Do Round 1 of Day 6 (page {{p:seven}}). It takes 15 minutes, and then you're back to the 2-minute wipe.

## System 7: The Bedroom & the Clothes Chair {.sys}

**The problem:** The chair (or the floor, or the end of the bed) is buried in clothes. The bed is never made. The nightstand is a museum of water glasses.

**The system:** a home for "worn once" clothes, a 30-second bed, and a nightstand tray.

**Set it up (15 minutes):**

- [ ] **A WEAR AGAIN home:** hooks on the wall or door, or one open basket.
- [ ] **A hamper** where you take clothes off (not across the room).
- [ ] **A tray** on the nightstand for the small bits.
- [ ] **A "bedroom exit" spot** by the door for cups and dishes that need to go to the kitchen in the morning.

**Keep it going:**

- **The 30-second bed:** pull up the duvet, toss the pillows. That's a made bed. (Many people skip the top sheet entirely. You're allowed.)
- **Clothes off, clothes placed:** hamper, WEAR AGAIN, or hanger. Three options, no thinking.
- **Morning cup run:** anything in the bedroom exit spot goes to the kitchen when you do.

**When it breaks:** the chair fills up again? That's normal. Once a week, do Round 2 of Day 5 (page {{p:seven}}): three piles, 12 minutes. Some weeks the chair wins. It's still a chair.

:::script Say it like this
*"The chair isn't a failure. It's a signal that my clothes need an easier home."*
:::

# The 5-Bin Declutter
<!--meta
id: declutter
color: butter
tagline: Five bins, ten seconds per thing, zero arguments with a sock.
one: **Work in 10-minute rounds on one small area** (a shelf, not a room). Pick up each thing, decide in 10 seconds, and drop it in one of five bins: **Trash, Donate, Elsewhere, Keep, Maybe.** Stuck? Use a decision script, or it goes in Maybe. Trash and donations leave the house the same day.
-->

Decluttering is where a lot of us get stuck, because every single object asks for a decision, and decisions are the most tiring part. The 5-bin method solves that with **pre-made decisions**: only five possible answers, and a script for every hard one.

## The five bins

<div class="bins">
<div class="bin b-trash"><b>TRASH</b><span>Broken, expired, stained, mystery cables with no device. Also recycling.</span></div>
<div class="bin b-donate"><b>DONATE</b><span>Good condition, but not for you anymore. Someone else will love it.</span></div>
<div class="bin b-else"><b>ELSEWHERE</b><span>Keeping it, but it lives in another room. Delivered at the end.</span></div>
<div class="bin b-keep"><b>KEEP</b><span>Stays here, and has (or gets) a home in this space.</span></div>
<div class="bin b-maybe"><b>MAYBE</b><span>Can't decide in 10 seconds. Box it, date it, decide later (or never).</span></div>
</div>

Use boxes, bags, laundry baskets, anything. Label them with masking tape. **Labels matter:** when you're mid-round and tired, you want to drop, not think.

## How to do a round

1. **Pick a tiny area.** One shelf. One drawer. One corner of the counter. **Not a room.** Rooms are for Future You.
2. **Set a timer for 10 minutes.** Put on music.
3. **Pick up one thing.** Decide in about 10 seconds. Drop it in a bin.
4. **Repeat** until the timer rings or the area is clear.
5. **Close out (5 minutes):** Trash out to the bin outside. Donate bag straight into your car or by the front door. Deliver ELSEWHERE. Date the MAYBE box and put it away.
6. **Celebrate.** Photo of the clear shelf. Yes, even a tiny shelf.

:::brain The Maybe box is a secret weapon
The Maybe box takes the pressure off. You don't have to decide *forever* right now. Write today's date on it and put it somewhere out of the way. **If you haven't opened it or missed anything in 60 days, donate it unopened.** Your brain already made the decision for you by forgetting it existed.
:::

## Decision scripts (for when you're stuck)

Hold the thing. Read one question. Answer honestly. Drop it in a bin. If none of these work in 10 seconds: Maybe.

<div class="scripts">
<div class="sc"><b>"If I lost this today, would I buy it again?"</b><span>No? Donate or trash.</span></div>
<div class="sc"><b>"Have I used this in the last year?"</b><span>No, and it's not seasonal or an emergency thing? Donate.</span></div>
<div class="sc"><b>"Do I have another one that's better?"</b><span>Keep the better one. Donate the spare.</span></div>
<div class="sc"><b>"Is this for the me I am, or the me I think I'll become someday?"</b><span>Keep things for the you who lives here now.</span></div>
<div class="sc"><b>"Am I keeping this out of guilt?"</b><span>Gifts, expensive mistakes. The money is already spent. Letting go doesn't waste it twice.</span></div>
<div class="sc"><b>"Would I pay to move this to a new home?"</b><span>If you'd leave it behind in a move, you can leave it behind now.</span></div>
<div class="sc"><b>"Does it have a home here?"</b><span>No home and no room for one? That's a strong vote for letting go.</span></div>
<div class="sc"><b>"Would I be happy if a friend took this?"</b><span>Yes? Donate. You've already let it go in your heart.</span></div>
</div>

## The tricky categories

**"But I might need it someday."** Try the *under-20 rule*: if you could replace it for less than about $20 in less than about 20 minutes, and you haven't used it in a year, it can go. You're trading a small, unlikely cost for a lot of space.

**Sentimental things.** Don't start here. Sentimental items are the hardest decisions of all, so save them until the easy stuff has warmed you up. When you do: keep the best few, take photos of the rest, and give the special ones a **memory box** with a size limit. The box decides how much you keep, not guilt.

**Gifts.** A gift's job was done the moment it was given. You're allowed to thank it and pass it on.

**The "I'll sell it" pile.** Selling takes photos, listings, messages, and meetups. That's a lot of steps for our brains. If you want to sell something, give it a **deadline**: listed within a week, sold within a month, or it goes to donate. No hard feelings.

**Hobby supplies for hobbies you don't do anymore.** Keep one small box for the hobby you're *actually* likely to pick up again. Donate the rest to a school, art program, or someone who's new to it.

**Other people's stuff.** Not yours to declutter. Put it in an ELSEWHERE pile for them, or ask. (See Chapter 11 for kind ways to talk about it.)

:::script Say it like this (to yourself, while holding a hard one)
*"Thank you for being part of my life. You can go be useful to someone else now."*

It sounds silly. It works more often than you'd think.
:::

## Getting it out the door

A donate bag sitting by your front door for three months is a doom pile in a bag.

- **Donations go in the car immediately.** Next time you drive past a donation spot, you're ready.
- **Trash leaves the same day.** All the way to the outside bin.
- **Set a "drop-off date"** in your phone for this week.
- **No car?** Many areas have charity pickups, buy-nothing groups, or neighbors happy to take things. One message, and it's gone.

# Energy Menus
<!--meta
id: energy
color: lilac
tagline: Match the task to the battery you have today, not the one you wish you had.
one: **Check your energy first, then pick from the matching menu.** Low energy? Choose a sit-down task. Medium? A 10-minute one. High? A timed round on something big. **Every menu item counts.** Doing a low-energy task on a low-energy day is the system working.
-->

Most cleaning plans assume you have the same energy every day. You don't. None of us do. Some days you could reorganize the garage. Some days, getting a glass of water feels like a mountain.

Energy menus let you **still make progress on every kind of day,** by picking from a list that matches what you actually have.

## Step one: check your battery

Before you start, ask: *"What's my battery at right now?"*

<div class="battery">
<div class="bat low"><span class="cells"><i></i></span><b>Low</b><em>Tired, foggy, sad, sick, overwhelmed. Sitting down sounds great.</em></div>
<div class="bat med"><span class="cells"><i></i><i></i></span><b>Medium</b><em>Okay-ish. Can do a thing or two if it's short.</em></div>
<div class="bat high"><span class="cells"><i></i><i></i><i></i></span><b>High</b><em>Restless, buzzy, motivated. Let's use it before it's gone.</em></div>
</div>

## The Low-Energy Menu

<p class="small">2 to 5 minutes each. Many can be done sitting down. Pick one. Any one.</p>

<div class="menu low" markdown="1">

- Put all the dishes within reach into one stack
- Throw away the trash you can reach from the couch
- Open the mail over a recycling bag
- Match socks while watching TV
- Wipe the bathroom sink with a wipe
- Put five things back where they belong
- Clear only the nightstand
- Fold one blanket and fluff the couch cushions
- Take the trash bag to the door (not outside, only the door)
- Unload only the cutlery from the dishwasher
- Make the bed the 30-second way
- Order groceries online instead of going out
- Put on clean pajamas and change the pillowcase
- Charge your phone at the launch pad
- Write tomorrow's one important thing on a sticky note
- Refill the soap
- Spray the shower and walk away (the spray does the work)
- Put your chaos basket by the stairs for tomorrow

</div>

## The Medium-Energy Menu

<p class="small">About 10 minutes each. Timer on, music on.</p>

<div class="menu med" markdown="1">

- The Kitchen Closing Shift
- The 10-Minute Tidy of one room
- Start a load of laundry and set the WET CLOTHES alarm
- Put away one basket of clean laundry
- Clear and wipe the dining table
- The bathroom 2-minute wipe, plus a toilet swish
- Declutter one drawer with the 5 bins
- Empty the fridge of anything expired
- Take out all the trash and recycling
- Empty the chaos basket or doom basket
- The 10-minute paper date
- Vacuum or sweep one room (only the middle, skip the corners)
- Wipe the stovetop and microwave
- Change the sheets (it counts as two tasks)

</div>

## The High-Energy Menu

<p class="small">Three or four 15-minute rounds, with breaks. Ride the wave, but stop before you crash.</p>

<div class="menu high" markdown="1">

- Any day from the 7-Day Reset
- Dismantle one doom pile
- Laundry to zero-ish (the full loop, with alarms)
- Declutter one closet shelf or one kitchen cabinet
- Clean out the car
- Deep clean one thing (the oven, the shower, the fridge shelves)
- Set up a new system from Chapter 6
- Take the donate bags to the drop-off
- Rearrange a space so things live where you use them
- The full Sunday Reset

</div>

:::brain Brain note: the high-energy crash
High-energy days are precious, and it's tempting to use every last drop. But doing *everything* often leads to a crash the next day, and then several days of nothing. Try this: **when you feel the energy start to dip, do one more small task, then stop.** Leave some battery for tomorrow.
:::

## Make it fun: the dopamine add-ons

Add one of these to any menu item to make it easier to start:

- **Race a song.** Can you finish before it ends?
- **The "only while cleaning" podcast.** A show you save exclusively for chores.
- **Call a friend** on speaker while you tidy.
- **A clean-with-me video** as a body double.
- **The reward first.** Pour the nice drink, then clean while you sip it.
- **Put on "going out" music** and pretend you're getting the house ready for a party.
- **Make it a game:** pick up 10 things of one color, or 15 things in 5 minutes.
- **Change the scenery:** new cleaning spray scent, a fun cloth, a timer that looks cute.

:::try Try this now
Copy the Low-Energy Menu onto a sticky note (or print the fridge version at the back of this book). The next time you feel stuck on the couch, pick one item. Only one.
:::

# The Sunday Reset
<!--meta

color: coral
tagline: One weekly hour (in pieces) that makes Monday feel possible.

The Daily 3 keeps things from snowballing. The Sunday Reset catches everything that slipped through the cracks during the week, and helps you walk into Monday feeling like you're on top of things. (It doesn't have to be Sunday. Choose any day that works.)

## The seven steps

<table class="timer">
<thead><tr><th>Step</th><th>What you do</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>1. Brain dump<br>5 min</td><td>Write down everything in your head: tasks, worries, things to buy, things to remember. It doesn't need to be organized. The goal is to get it out of your brain and onto paper.</td></tr>

## Make it something you look forward to

The Sunday Reset works best when it feels like a ritual, not a punishment. Some ideas:

- A special **"Sunday Reset" playlist** or podcast
- Your favorite **drink** or a **snack** that only exists on reset day
- A **candle** or fresh flowers once the house sweep is done
- A **body double**: a friend on video, doing their reset at the same time
- A **reward** at the end: a bath, a show, a walk, a nap

:::min The 15-minute mini reset
For hard weeks, do only three things: **(1)** a 5-minute brain dump, **(2)** a 5-minute house sweep with the chaos basket, **(3)** 5 minutes setting up Monday at the launch pad. That's a Sunday Reset. It counts.
:::

:::try Try this now
Pick your reset day and put a recurring reminder in your phone for it. Name it something kind, like *"Sunday Reset: future me says thanks."*
:::

# Fell Off the Wagon? The Restart Protocol
<!--meta
id: restart
color: sage
tagline: There is no wagon. There's only today, and one small thing.
one: **Don't catch up. Start from today.** Name what happened without shame, do one 15-minute reset of the kitchen, then do only the Daily 1 for three days before going back to the Daily 3. Every restart is practice, and you'll get faster at it every time.
-->

Let's get this out of the way: **you will fall off.** Everybody does. You'll get sick, or busy, or sad, or distracted by a new hobby, and one day you'll look up and the house will look like Week Zero again.

That's not the system failing. **Restarting *is* part of the system.** People with tidy homes aren't people who never fall off. They're people who get back on quickly. And getting back on is a skill you can practice.

## The 5-step restart

<ol class="bigsteps">
<li><strong>Name it, kindly.</strong> Say out loud (or in your head): <em>"Things slid. That makes sense. I'm restarting today."</em> No lecture. No autopsy. The house doesn't care why, and neither do we.</li>
<li><strong>Drop the backlog.</strong> You are not catching up on three weeks of chores. That list doesn't exist anymore. Today is Day 1 again, and Day 1 is small.</li>
<li><strong>Reset one room: the kitchen.</strong> Do the 15-minute kitchen reset (Day 1 minimum version, page {{p:seven}}). The kitchen is the fastest way to make the whole house feel like it's back under control.</li>
<li><strong>Three days of the Daily 1.</strong> Only dishes in. Nothing else required. Three days in a row gets momentum rolling without draining your battery.</li>
<li><strong>Then add the Daily 3 back.</strong> One reset at a time if you like. Add the Sunday Reset when it feels possible.</li>
</ol>

:::script Say it like this (to yourself)
*"I'm not starting over. I'm starting again, with everything I learned last time."*
:::

## Find the leak (only if you want to)

Once you're back on, it can help to notice *what* knocked the system over. Not to blame yourself, but to patch the hole for next time.

<table class="compare">
<thead><tr><th>What happened</th><th>Try this next time</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>I got sick or had a hard week</td><td>Switch straight to the Daily 1 the moment you notice. Plan for "hard-week mode" (below).</td></tr>
<tr><td>My schedule changed (new job, holidays, travel)</td><td>Re-glue your Daily 3 to new habits. The old glue stopped working.</td></tr>
<tr><td>I got bored of it</td><td>Change the flavor: new playlist, body double, timer, or reward. Same system, new packaging.</td></tr>
<tr><td>One zone kept overflowing</td><td>That zone needs a system or a bigger home. Check Chapter 6.</td></tr>
<tr><td>I tried to do too much and crashed</td><td>Go back to minimum versions for a week. Small and steady wins.</td></tr>
<tr><td>I honestly don't know</td><td>That's okay. You don't need to know to restart.</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

## Hard-week mode

Some weeks aren't about a tidy home. They're about getting through. Illness, grief, burnout, a new baby, a big deadline. For those weeks, the goal shrinks to **safe and sanitary**, and everything else is optional.

:::min Hard-week permission slip
**You are allowed to:**

- Use paper plates and disposable cups
- Live out of the clean laundry basket
- Order groceries, order food, eat cereal for dinner
- Close the doors of rooms you can't deal with
- Ask someone for help (see the scripts in the next chapter)
- Do only the Daily 1, or nothing, and restart when the week is over

**The only goals:** trash out, food not spoiling, and a clear spot to sleep.
:::

:::brain A gentle note
If the mess is part of a bigger feeling (like everything being hard, all the time, for a long time), you deserve support with that too. A doctor, therapist, or someone you trust is a great place to start. Needing help isn't a failure. It's a smart move.
:::

## Why every restart is easier than the last

The first time you do the 7-Day Reset, you're building systems from nothing. The second time, the launch pad already exists. The laundry alarms are already in your phone. The Maybe box is already dated.

**Every restart starts from a higher floor.** You never go all the way back to zero. Even on the messiest day, you know how to get out.

# Living With Other Humans
<!--meta
id: others
color: butter
tagline: Partners, roommates, and kids. Systems beat nagging, every time.
one: **Make the system do the asking.** Labels, visible homes, and simple shared resets help everyone put things away without anyone nagging. Agree on a small "shared space baseline," divide jobs by ownership (not "helping"), and use kind, specific scripts when you need to talk about it.
-->

Other people make everything more complicated. They have their own stuff, their own standards, and their own brains. Sometimes you're the messy one. Sometimes they are. Often, it's both of you, in different ways.

The good news: **everything in this book works better with more people, not worse.** Labels, visible homes, and short timed resets are easy for anyone to follow, including kids and people who swear they "don't see mess."

## Four principles for a shared home

1. **Systems beat nagging.** A labeled basket that says SHOES reminds people to put their shoes away without you saying a word. Every time you catch yourself reminding someone of the same thing, ask: *"What system could remind them instead?"*
2. **Agree on a baseline, not a standard.** People will never agree on "clean." But you can agree on a short list of things that are true in shared spaces most of the time.
3. **Own, don't help.** "Can you help with the dishes?" makes one person the manager. "You own the kitchen closing shift on weeknights" gives someone a whole job, from start to finish.
4. **Assume a different brain, not bad intentions.** Your partner leaving socks on the floor is probably not a message. It's probably a missing hamper.

## The shared space baseline

Sit down together for 15 minutes (with snacks) and agree on **three to five things** that will be true in shared spaces by the end of each day. For example:

- [ ] No dishes left outside the kitchen overnight
- [ ] Kitchen closing shift done every night (rotating)
- [ ] Shoes and bags on the launch pad, not the floor
- [ ] Living room floor clear before bed
- [ ] Bathroom counter wiped after the evening routine

**Keep it short.** Five things everyone can do beats twenty that nobody does. Write the list down and stick it somewhere visible.

## Scripts for partners

:::script When you want to bring it up (without a fight)
*"Hey, I've been reading about ways to make the house easier for both of us. Can we pick a time this week to talk about it for 15 minutes? I'll bring snacks."*
:::

:::script When you're the messier one
*"I know my stuff ends up everywhere, and I'm working on it. It's not that I don't care. My brain really doesn't see it. Would it help if we set up a basket for my things in the living room, so there's one spot instead of ten?"*
:::

:::script When they're the messier one
*"I've noticed your [bag / shoes / mugs] end up in [place] a lot. What if we made that their official spot? I'll put a basket there. Would that work for you?"*
:::

:::script When you need help on a hard day
*"I'm running on empty today. Could you take the kitchen tonight? I'll take it tomorrow."*
:::

## Roommates

Roommates are trickier, because you're sharing space without sharing a life. Keep things clear and light:

- **Divide by zone, not by task.** "You own the bathroom, I own the kitchen" is easier than splitting every chore.
- **Rotate monthly** so nobody gets stuck with the worst job forever.
- **Label shared shelves** in the fridge and cupboards. Clear boundaries are kind.
- **Weekly 10-minute check-in** (or a group chat reminder) instead of passive-aggressive notes.

:::script Say it like this (to a roommate)
*"Hey, I'm trying out a new cleaning system and it'd be easier with a plan. Want to pick zones? I'm happy to take the kitchen if you take the bathroom, and we swap next month."*
:::

## Kids

Kids are mess-makers by design, but they're also surprisingly good at systems when the systems are simple and visible.

- **Picture labels on bins** for kids who can't read yet. A photo of a car on the car bin.
- **Fewer toys out at once.** Rotate a few bins every couple of weeks. Less stuff means less mess, and "old" toys feel new again.
- **Make tidy-up a race.** One song, everyone tidies, the house is reset before the song ends.
- **Pair jobs with routines:** shoes on the launch pad when you walk in, dirty clothes in the hamper at bath time.

<table class="compare">
<thead><tr><th>Age (roughly)</th><th>Jobs they can often own</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>3 to 5</td><td>Toys into bins, shoes to the launch pad, clothes into the hamper, wipe the table with help</td></tr>
<tr><td>6 to 9</td><td>Make their bed (30-second style), set and clear the table, match socks, feed a pet, empty small trash cans</td></tr>
<tr><td>10 to 13</td><td>Load or unload the dishwasher, run a load of laundry with you, the 10-Minute Tidy of their room</td></tr>
<tr><td>14+</td><td>Own a full zone or a nightly kitchen closing shift, do their own laundry loop</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

Every kid is different (and some kids have ADHD brains too). Adjust freely. The goal is **ownership, not perfection.**

:::script Say it like this (to kids)
*"It's reset time! Let's see if we can get every toy in its bin before this song ends. Ready? Go!"*

And after: *"Look at that floor! You did that."*
:::

## When nobody agrees

Sometimes, despite everyone's best efforts, you'll have different ideas of what "clean" means. A few things that help:

- **Give each person one "no-rules zone"** (a desk, a corner, their side of the bedroom) where their own standards apply.
- **Focus on shared spaces only.** What happens in someone's own room is their business.
- **Try the "whoever cares most" deal:** if you care about something more (say, a clear dining table), it can be your zone, and they take something else.
- **Outsource if you can,** even one deep clean a season. It can save a lot of arguments.

- **Visible beats hidden.** Labels do the remembering.
